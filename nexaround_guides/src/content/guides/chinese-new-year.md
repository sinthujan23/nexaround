## What happens

- **New Year's Eve**: families gather for the **reunion dinner**, the most important meal of the year. Homes are cleaned beforehand to sweep out bad luck, and doors are decorated with red paper and couplets.
- **New Year's Day**: visits to family and temples, and **red envelopes** (*hongbao*, *lai see*) of money for children and unmarried relatives. Many people avoid sweeping or washing hair on this day, so as not to wash away good luck.
- **The first days**: **lion and dragon dances** in the streets, shopping centres and temples, firecrackers where they're allowed, and fairs.
- **The 15th day**: the **Lantern Festival**, with lanterns, riddles and sweet rice balls (*tangyuan*), closes the season.

Each year belongs to one of the 12 animals of the Chinese zodiac: 2027 is the **Goat**, 2028 the **Monkey** and 2029 the **Rooster**.

## Where to see it

- **Hong Kong**: the **night parade** in Tsim Sha Tsui on New Year's Day, with free viewing along the route; **fireworks over Victoria Harbour** on the second day, usually at 8 pm; and the huge **flower fair in Victoria Park**, Causeway Bay, open until the morning of New Year's Day.
- **Singapore**: **Chinatown** is lit with lanterns and street lights for weeks, with markets selling New Year food and decorations.
- **Penang, Malaysia**: **Kek Lok Si**, a vast hillside temple, is lit up with thousands of lanterns over the New Year period.
- **Beijing**: traditional **temple fairs**, such as the one at **Ditan Park**, with snacks, performances and red lanterns hanging from the trees.

## The travel rush

The weeks around New Year are the **Chunyun**, the biggest yearly migration on Earth, as people travel home to their families. China's 2026 rush lasted 40 days and counted a record **9.41 billion** trips, most of them by road.

- **Trains and flights** within China sell out fast and cost more. Book as early as tickets go on sale, or travel within China after the first week.
- **Many shops, restaurants and offices close** for the first days of the holiday, especially in mainland China. Big cities can feel quiet while families are at home; tourist sites and temples are busy.
- **Hotels** in Hong Kong, Singapore and popular cities fill up and raise prices: book well ahead.

## Etiquette

- **Red** is lucky; avoid giving white flowers or anything in sets of four, a number that sounds like "death" in Chinese.
- If you're invited to a family, bring a gift such as fruit, and give and receive things with both hands.
- At temples, follow what others do, don't step on the raised thresholds, and ask before photographing worshippers.
