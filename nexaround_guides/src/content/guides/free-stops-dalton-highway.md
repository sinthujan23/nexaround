## Free camping: the rules

Since 2022 and 2023 the BLM has charged **$12 a night** at three of its four Dalton campgrounds: Arctic Circle (MP 115), Marion Creek (MP 180) and Galbraith Lake (MP 275). **Five Mile (MP 60) still has no camping fee.** All four are open roughly May to September, first come, first served.

Outside the campgrounds, camping on BLM land is free and allowed for **up to 14 days at any one spot**. People camp in pull-outs and old gravel pits. Park well off the road, never block an access road to the pipeline, and take everything out with you: the BLM hauls about 20 tons of litter off the Dalton every year.

## Yukon Crossing (MP 56)

The Dalton's bridge over the Yukon River is the only road bridge across the river in Alaska. Just before it, you drive under the pipeline to the BLM's **Yukon Crossing contact station**, open in summer, with information on the road ahead, and a wooden overlook with panels about the river and the bridge. Fuel, food and rooms are at the Yukon River Camp, by the bridge.

## Five Mile Campground (MP 60)

The last free campground on the Dalton, four miles north of the river: seven gravel sites, each with a picnic table and fire ring, a vault toilet, and **drinking water from an artesian well**, the only reliable tap for a long way. There's also the only RV dump station on the highway, which costs $5.

## Finger Mountain (MP 98)

A wayside with parking, toilets and information boards, and short, unmaintained trails across the tundra to the **granite tors** around Finger Mountain, rocks that rise above the scrub like ruins. The finger-shaped tor was once a landmark for pilots flying to Fairbanks. In summer the toilets are open; in winter they close.

## Arctic Circle wayside (MP 115)

The famous sign, a viewing deck and picnic tables at the line of 66°33′ North. Stopping here is free. The campground half a mile up the hill costs $12 a night and has no water.

## Gobblers Knob (MP 132)

A hilltop rest stop and viewpoint marked on the BLM's Dalton map, 17 miles north of the Arctic Circle: a good place to stretch your legs before the long run to Coldfoot.

## Arctic Interagency Visitor Center (MP 175)

In Coldfoot, the **Arctic Interagency Visitor Center** is free: exhibits and films on the Brooks Range and the people and wildlife of the far north, nature trails outside, evening programmes, and rangers who can tell you about the road, the weather and the land on both sides of it. It opens in summer only, roughly late May to mid-September; check this year's hours before you rely on it. Coldfoot is also your last fuel for 240 miles.

## Sukakpak Mountain (MP 204)

The highway runs right past **Sukakpak Mountain**, a pale wall of limestone partly turned to marble by heat and pressure. Look for **palsas** at its foot: low mounds of earth pushed up by ice inside them. The name is said to mean "marten deadfall", after the trap the mountain resembles from the north. Use the pull-out west of the mountain.

## Chandalar Shelf (MP 238)

Past the last spruce trees, the road climbs onto the **Chandalar Shelf** before the final pull up to Atigun Pass. The views back down the Dietrich River valley are some of the best on the road, and caribou cross here.

## Atigun Pass (MP 244)

At **4,739 ft**, the highest pass in Alaska kept open all year, and the crossing of the Continental Divide. Snow can fall here in any month. The grades are steep: never stop on the slope itself, only in pull-outs, and give trucks plenty of room.

## Sag River Overlook (MP 348)

Far out on the North Slope, a BLM viewpoint looks over the braided channels of the **Sagavanirktok River** and the flat tundra running north towards Prudhoe Bay. Watch for muskoxen.

## Before you go

None of these stops sells anything. Fill up at every fuel stop (the Yukon River crossing, Coldfoot and Deadhorse), carry water and food, and check **511.alaska.gov** for road conditions.
