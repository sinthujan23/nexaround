## 1. Maps that work offline

**Download before you lose signal**, while you're still on hotel Wi-Fi:

- **Google Maps** lets you download areas and navigate offline. Download the whole route plus a wide margin either side. In some countries offline download isn't available; check before you rely on it.
- **OpenStreetMap-based apps** such as Organic Maps or OsmAnd download whole regions and work fully offline, including routing. They often show tracks that Google doesn't.
- **Save the places that matter as pins:** every fuel stop, where you're sleeping, hospitals or clinics, and police posts.
- **Screenshot or print** bookings, permits and directions to your first night's stop.

Carry a paper map too. It never runs out of battery.

## 2. Don't let the app choose the road

Navigation apps are built for places with good data. Out here, they can be dangerously wrong:

- **In Yakutia in 2020**, a young driver followed Google Maps onto the abandoned Old Summer Road in winter and froze to death. That road's bridges have been gone for years.
- **When we measured routes for this site**, Google Maps placed three Nullarbor stops at points away from the highway, adding nearly 90 km to the Adelaide–Perth total. In Yakutia it offered a detour of more than 1,100 km because it didn't know a road existed.

The fix: **plan the route yourself**, with waypoints on exact places (a roadhouse's pin, not a town name), check it against a paper map or a guide, and **ask locals** about road conditions before each leg. If the app suddenly wants you on a smaller road, stop and find out why.

## 3. Fuel: plan for the longest gap

1. **Find the longest stretch without fuel** on your route. On the Nullarbor it's 191 km (Balladonia to Norseman); in the Pamirs and on the Kolyma Highway, stations can be much further apart and may be closed.
2. **Work out your real range:** tank size divided by consumption. Then knock off a third for headwinds, heat with the air-conditioning on, heavy loads and gravel.
3. **Fill up at every stop,** even half-full, and carry spare fuel where it's legal and safe, so you can always cover two legs in case a pump is dry.

## 4. Water and food

NSW National Parks advises carrying **at least 4 litres of water per person per day**, plus a **20-litre emergency reserve**. In real heat, carry more. Pack food that needs no cooking for two extra days.

## 5. A way to call for help without signal

You need at least one of these on any truly remote road:

- **Satellite SOS on your phone.** Recent iPhones can send emergency texts by satellite in a growing list of countries (Norway was added in September 2026), and some recent Android phones offer something similar. It needs a clear view of the sky, and it only works where it's supported. Check before you go.
- **A satellite messenger.** A small device that sends texts and an SOS by satellite almost anywhere. It needs a subscription, but lets you send "all fine" messages too.
- **A personal locator beacon (PLB).** A 406 MHz beacon sends your position to search-and-rescue services worldwide, with no subscription. Register it with your country's authority (in Australia, AMSA; registration is free). Older 121.5 MHz-only beacons are no longer detected in Australia.

Set off only after you've **told someone your route and when to expect you**, and agreed what they'll do if you don't check in.

## 6. If the car stops

- **Stay with your vehicle.** It's far easier to spot from the air than a person walking. Raise the bonnet, and put out something bright or reflective.
- **Stay in the shade, and ration your sweat, not your water.** Drink what you need.
- **Use your beacon or satellite SOS** if anyone's life or health is at risk. That is what they're for.

## 7. Before you leave town

- Service the car, and carry **a good spare tyre** (two for long gravel roads), a puncture repair kit and a pump.
- **Don't drive at dawn, dusk or night** on outback roads, when animals are on the move.
- Charge everything, and carry a power bank and a car charger.
- Check the weather and road conditions for the next leg, every morning.
